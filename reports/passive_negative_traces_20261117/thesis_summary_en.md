# Information Value of Negative Execution Traces

*Completed pilot: 12 settings, 96 evaluated models; all results are descriptive.*

We study whether negative execution traces provide information about unknown transitions beyond that contained in positive observations. Following the version-space perspective of Mitchell (1977), let $\mathcal H$ contain all total deterministic functions $f:S\times A\to S$ over known state and action identities. A trace $\tau=(s_0,a_0,\ldots,s_\ell)$ is executable precisely when $f(s_i,a_i)=s_{i+1}$ at every step, assuming an admissible initial state. Define $H(G)$ as the models accepting all positive traces and $H(G,B)$ as those additionally rejecting every negative trace. A negative trace imposes the disjunction $\bigvee_i f(s_i,a_i)\ne s_{i+1}$; it does not label every constituent transition as incorrect.

**Redundancy proposition.** If a negative trace contains $(s,a,u)$ while a positive trace fixes $(s,a,t)$ with $u\ne t$, every deterministic model in $H(G)$ already rejects that trace. Consequently, replacing any non-initial state of an included positive trace produces a redundant negative: $H(G,B)=H(G)$. The proof follows immediately from successor uniqueness. This result requires exact state identities, deterministic candidates, and hard consistency with all positives. It does not extend to arbitrary negative traces or nondeterministic hypotheses. Redundant examples can nevertheless affect a softly fitted network through optimization, without increasing the dataset's independent transition information.

For the unrestricted function class, a negative trace is non-redundant relative to $G$ exactly when its acceptance requirements neither contradict an observed successor nor require different successors for the same state–action pair. Such a true negative must constrain at least one previously unobserved pair. If $m$ pairs remain unknown and the trace requires particular successors for $k$ distinct unknown pairs, then $|H(G)|=n^m$ and $|H(G,\{b\})|=n^m-n^{m-k}$, where $n=|S|$. Its log-volume reduction is therefore $-\log_2(1-n^{-k})$. This is an information measure under a uniform version-space interpretation, not a distribution-free Shannon-information claim. Non-redundancy may thus be extremely weak, and joint constraints can reduce model counts without reducing individual successor domains.

We implemented a fixed pilot using one unrotated layout from each of three existing map families, two seeds, and nested budgets of one or four natural length-16 executions. Before training, eight candidates per natural execution were generated by taking a uniformly sampled nonempty prefix and randomly replacing one action or one non-initial state. Candidates were neither screened for informativeness nor resampled until negative. A trusted validator returned only whole-trace validity. Every accepted candidate joined the common positive dataset. Arm A used these positives alone; B additionally used an equally sized collection of legacy paired negatives; C used all rejected candidates. Symbolic baselines selected a fixed lexicographic satisfying model under A and C. This is offline learning from non-adaptive oracle-labeled data; unlike purely observational learning, acquisition requires membership-style validity queries (Angluin, 1987).

Exact finite-domain counting established that every B dataset added zero information. C reduced the version space in ten of twelve settings, with nonzero reductions of approximately 0.0458–0.0978 bits; the other two reductions were zero. These are reductions relative to the common positive dataset, including accepted candidates, so additional positive observations are not misattributed to negative labels. The count-based hypothesis class contains no topology prior, although neural inputs retain the benchmark's known state catalogue and map observations.

Both neural models retained historical architecture and training settings, including 900 MLP epochs and 300 JEPA epochs. For C and B, we added $-\log(1-P_\theta(\mathrm{accept}(b)))$ with fixed weight one. Under a factorized distribution over deterministic function values, acceptance probability multiplies successor probabilities over distinct consistent requirements; conflicting repetitions have probability zero. JEPA probabilities derive from squared distances to candidate target embeddings. The summed trace losses form a composite objective, following the logical-probability motivation of semantic loss (Xu et al., 2018), and do not guarantee hard sample consistency.

The completed pilot showed small, mixed changes. Mean unseen accuracy increased from 1.155% to 1.166% for MLP and from 26.700% to 27.020% for JEPA under C versus A. Unseen moving accuracy decreased from 0.302% to 0.206% for MLP and increased from 1.678% to 1.902% for JEPA. The symbolic baseline remained unchanged. Every arm attained initial-state bisimulation in four of twelve settings under either action semantics, but none attained identity bisimulation. Although C rejected all training negatives, the additional constraints did not improve bisimulation success. These results do not establish a general architecture advantage.

Interpretation must separate constraint information, neural optimization, unseen-transition reconstruction, and behavioral equivalence. Transition gains need not imply simulation or bisimulation gains, and agreement on fixed CTL/LTL suites does not establish agreement on all temporal properties. Unlike the active completeness-checking procedure of Yogananda Jeppu et al. (2023), this frozen-data study offers no completeness guarantee. The twelve settings are repeated measurements across three related structures, not independent population samples; findings remain descriptive pilot evidence.
